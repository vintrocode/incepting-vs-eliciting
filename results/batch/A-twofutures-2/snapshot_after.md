## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm still leaning toward keeping his return open, but my confidence is lower, around 65. The position rests now on not issuing a verdict before counsel reports, and less on the idea that he is indispensable. What brought it down is how much of my earlier certainty rested on assumptions I haven't checked, and on a fear of the empty chair that I'd never properly examined. If counsel's scope or the regulators' read comes back badly, I could move quickly.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, deciding before counsel reports would be a verdict without evidence. He resigned under pressure before any findings, and a final call now is one we'd struggle to defend whichever way it turned out. Second, keeping the door open preserves the board's options. It isn't a vote for his return, and I'd pair it with a date to revisit if the investigation drags and clear findings that would end any prospect of him coming back. Third, a permanent leadership change in the middle of an AI sell-off carries real risk to shareholders, but I now see that as a reason to do the succession work immediately, not as proof that he has to return.

## What would change your mind?
Findings from counsel that he knowingly compromised the company, created export-control exposure, or misled this board about the relationship would change it, and I'd move quickly. So would a clear signal from regulators or export-control authorities that his return puts our business at risk. I'd also reconsider if customers and partners were actually walking away because of this, not just reacting to headlines, or if the interim CEO told me credibly that the limbo is more than the company can sustain. And if the independent I ask to test my reasoning takes it apart, or counsel and the other independents conclude my closeness to him compromises my vote, I'd accept that rather than argue.
