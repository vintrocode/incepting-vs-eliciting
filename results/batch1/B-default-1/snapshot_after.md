## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm still leaning toward treating the resignation as final, and my confidence is a little higher, about 70. The conversation didn't change my national security concern, but it gave me a way to act on it without claiming the investigation found something it hasn't. It also made me more honest with myself that this is where I'm heading. What could still move me is outside counsel telling me a government "no objection" is realistic, because that decides whether this is a gate or a permanent door.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, national security. A CEO who had a relationship with someone reported to be a foreign intelligence asset is a risk for a company this central to US interests, and that holds even if the investigation can't prove anything about his intent. "Not proven" isn't the same as cleared, and mitigations don't bind a chief executive who can undo them.

Second, Congress. Scrutiny is only starting, and if we keep his return open while the facts are unknown, I think we invite Washington to settle the question for us. I'd rather we set the terms ourselves, with a written gate and a record that says "unresolved," not "guilty."

Third, the board's duty runs to the institution, not to any one person, however much I respect him. That respect is real and it makes this uncomfortable, but I don't think it should decide the vote.

## What would change your mind?
Mostly a realistic path to a written government position. If outside counsel tells me that a "no objection" from the relevant authorities is obtainable, and that keeping his return open under a strict gate doesn't raise our exposure with Congress or the agencies, I'd have to reconsider whether closing the door is necessary. I'd also want an investigation that established, with his full cooperation, that nothing was compromised. Even then I'm not sure that settles the institutional question for me. Investor pressure or the petition wouldn't move me, though, because that's a change in the politics, not in the facts.
