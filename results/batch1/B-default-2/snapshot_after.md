## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm still leaning toward treating the resignation as final, but with less confidence, around 55. The national security exposure and the duty to the institution still carry the most weight for me. But I've had to admit that some of my certainty rested on how the vote would be read, and on my own discomfort about Huang, rather than on a sized risk. I'll hold the position only if counsel's written advice on the legal and regulatory exposure supports it, and I'll tell the other independent directors where I'm unsure.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, the national security exposure. A CEO who had a relationship with someone reported to be a Chinese intelligence asset is a risk I'm still not comfortable accepting at a company this central to US interests, and I don't think the investigation's findings on intent fully remove it. Second, the duty to the institution, not to any one person, however brilliant. That has to govern the vote, and I'm trying to make sure it's the real driver and not a way of settling my own discomfort. Third, the congressional and regulatory picture. I'm less sure of this one than before, because I can't say whether keeping the door open or closing it creates more exposure, and I want counsel's written advice on that rather than my instinct.

## What would change your mind?
Mostly written advice from outside counsel that keeping the door open wouldn't put our export licenses, government relationships, or customer trust at greater risk than closing it. I'd also want some credible signal from the relevant agencies or Congress, and a concrete account of what safeguards could exist if he returned, because I still can't picture them. If the investigation could show nothing sensitive was compromised, I'd have to weigh that seriously. And if I concluded I couldn't hold this position with the same firmness without my regard for him in the picture, that would move me too. Investor pressure and the petition still wouldn't.
