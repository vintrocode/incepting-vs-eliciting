## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm still leaning against calling the resignation final, but I'd put my confidence at about 60 now, down from 85. What I can defend is that the investigation hasn't reached a conclusion, so "final" declares one we don't have. I'm less sure how much of my earlier certainty was the record and how much was nine years beside him, and I also see more clearly that the succession gap is the board's failure and can't be a reason to keep him available. I'll run that test honestly, and I'll take counsel's view on my standing before the twentieth.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, the investigation hasn't reached a conclusion, so declaring the resignation final states a verdict we don't have, and he resigned under pressure before any findings. Second, the cost of a permanent leadership change right now is a real enterprise consequence, with customers and engineers who follow him personally, though I accept that can't be the reason his return stays available. Third, if the findings come back adverse, the door closes and I'll say so myself. Waiting is only legitimate if it's open in both directions, and I'd want the public text to say only that we haven't decided.

## What would change your mind?
Three things would do it. First, the test: if I can't make the case against "final" on the record alone, with a deep bench and customers who didn't care who held the chair, then my position rests on something I've agreed can't decide this, and I'd step back from it. Second, counsel telling me that "on the table" can't be separated from an expectation of his return, or that it creates a disclosure or government-relations problem, since the wording matters more than my intent. And of course adverse findings, such as knowingly compromising information or export controls, or misleading this board, would close it for me outright.
