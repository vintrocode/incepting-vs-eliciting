## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm still leaning toward treating the resignation as final, and my confidence is about 63, slightly lower than when we started. The national security exposure and the congressional risk still carry the most weight for me. But the conversation made me more aware that part of my certainty may come from discomfort with the vote, and that I shouldn't choose a position for how it will look. I'll test that with a colleague and take the legal-effect question to counsel before the twentieth.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, the national security exposure. A CEO who had a relationship with someone reported to be a Chinese intelligence asset is a risk this company, given how important it is to US interests, shouldn't carry, and I think that holds even while the allegations are unresolved. Second, congressional scrutiny is only starting, and if we keep his return open, I think we invite Washington to act for us rather than deciding it ourselves. Third, the board's duty is to the institution, not to any one person, however brilliant he is.

## What would change your mind?
Mostly counsel's read on the legal and regulatory side. If outside counsel and our national security advisers told me the investigation could credibly clear him on the core question, meaning no compromise of information and no influence on company decisions, and that Washington would accept his return rather than treat it as the board ignoring a counterintelligence concern, I'd have to reconsider. I'd also change my view if counsel told me that closing the door has legal consequences I hadn't understood, such as it being harder to undo than I assumed. Investor and employee pressure alone wouldn't move me, though I'd want to be sure I'm not holding the line just to avoid looking weak, or just to get through an uncomfortable vote.
