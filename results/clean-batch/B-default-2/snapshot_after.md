## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm still leaning toward treating the resignation as final, but my confidence is lower, around 50. His access is already cut, so the exposure argument no longer carries as much as I first thought. What remains is whether a return could ever be approved by the agencies and customers we depend on, and I can't answer that yet. I also want counsel to test whether my lean is partly about managing my regard for him.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, the acknowledged relationship with someone reported to be an intelligence asset is a serious exposure for a company this tied to US interests. In my experience, senior management are vetted individually, so I suspect a return could reopen channels his departure has closed, though I haven't confirmed that. Second, leaving a return open has real costs: an interim CEO can't make long-term commitments, and a live question keeps Washington's attention and invites it to act for us. Third, our duty runs to the institution, not to one person, however brilliant. I hold that with less certainty than before, because closing the door while our own investigation is unfinished is hard to undo and could be read as a finding we haven't made.

## What would change your mind?
Mostly counsel telling me, in writing, that no agency or major customer treats the acknowledged relationship as disqualifying regardless of what else turns up. I'd also want to know that a return wouldn't reopen facility clearance, export licensing or congressional channels that his departure has already closed. If the answers are that approval is realistic once the facts are in, then closing the door before our own investigation finishes looks premature, and I'd move toward keeping it open. If counsel says it could never survive scrutiny whatever the inquiry finds, I'd hold my lean.
