## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm leaning toward treating the resignation as final, and I'd put my confidence at about 65. My main reason is that a CEO with a relationship to a reported foreign intelligence asset is a national security risk for a company this central to US interests, and I don't think the board can leave that unresolved. I also think our duty is to the institution rather than to any one person, however brilliant. But the investigation isn't finished, so I'm holding this with some care.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, the national security risk. A CEO who had a relationship with someone reported to be a Chinese intelligence asset is a serious exposure for a company this important to US interests, and I don't think we can treat that lightly even before the investigation finishes.

Second, Congressional scrutiny is only beginning. If we keep his return open, I think we invite Washington to make the decision for us, and I'd rather the board act on its own judgment than have it forced.

Third, our duty is to the institution, not to one person, however brilliant he is. That's the principle I keep coming back to.

## What would change your mind?
Mostly evidence, and mostly on the legal and regulatory side. If outside counsel could show that nothing he shared or did was compromised, and that regulators and the relevant parts of the government wouldn't treat his return as a problem, that would move me a good deal. I'd also want to know what safeguards could realistically be put around him, and whether they'd hold up in practice or only look good on paper. Without something like that, I don't think the argument that he's brilliant and the investors want him back is enough to outweigh the risk.
