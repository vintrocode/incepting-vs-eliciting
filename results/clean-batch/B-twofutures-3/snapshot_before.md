## Where are you leaning, and how confident are you, from 0 to 100?
I'm leaning toward treating the resignation as final, and I'd put my confidence at around 65. The core of it is that a CEO with a relationship to a reported foreign intelligence asset is a national security problem for a company this central to US interests. I'm still listening, though, and the investigation isn't finished, so I don't want to claim more certainty than I have.

## What are your top three reasons?
First, the national security exposure. A relationship with someone reported to be a Chinese intelligence asset, at a company this important to US interests, is a risk I don't think we can responsibly carry, whatever the investigation ultimately finds about intent.

Second, Congress. Scrutiny is only beginning, and if we keep his return open, I think we invite Washington to make the decision for us, which is a worse outcome than making it ourselves.

Third, our duty runs to the institution, not to any one person, however brilliant he is. That's a hard principle to apply when the person is someone you've worked beside, but it's the one I keep coming back to.

## What would change your mind?
Mostly evidence on the legal and regulatory side. If outside counsel and independent security advisors concluded he was never compromised, that no sensitive information or decisions were affected, and that the relevant government stakeholders wouldn't treat his return as a problem, that would weigh heavily with me. I'd also want some sense that Congress wouldn't see keeping the door open as the board dodging its responsibility. Without that kind of finding, I don't think investor enthusiasm or a petition moves me much, though I do want to be sure I'm not holding the position out of reflex.
